I build machine learning systems, and study what they do after they ship.

A model that scores people also changes the people it will score next time. That loop is where disparity accumulates, and it is invisible to any single-snapshot fairness check.

My master's research at Case Western Reserve University built CALM, a framework for finding those loops in a deployed system, testing whether they compound, and finding a configuration that settles them. I defended it in August 2026.

Research

Feedback Loop Intervention for Long-Term Fairness in ML-Enabled Systems

May 2025 to Aug 2026

MS thesis, Case Western Reserve University. Advised by Prof. Sumon Biswas. Defended August 2026.

A lending model that declines a group receives fewer repayment records from that group, scores it lower next cycle, and declines more of it. Every individual decision is defensible. The composition is a ratchet. Most fairness work audits the decision rule. CALM treats the ratchet itself as the object of study.

  • Recovers the feedback structure of a running system from its own execution traces, without a hand-drawn causal graph.
  • Tests whether that structure amplifies over a long horizon or settles, which separates a system that is drifting from one that is merely noisy.
  • Searches the system's existing configuration space for a setting that damps the loop, then re-validates it on held-out simulation seeds so the search is never allowed to certify itself.
  • Returns a configuration a designer could already have set, which makes the result deployable rather than advisory.

Across four simulated decision systems, the long-run disparity gap fell by 27% to 76% against the no-intervention baseline, with utility held within tolerance.

The thesis also covers a safety analysis of feedback-coupled decision systems and a study of where language-model priors help, and where they mislead, in recovering causal structure. Methods and full results are in the thesis; a condensed version is under review.
